Incremental slip rates

A flight of dated markers records a slip history, not a rate. Getting a history out of them means throwing most of your random draws away, and the fraction you keep turns out to be one of the most informative numbers in the analysis.

Why one rate is not enough

Suppose you have four dated offset markers rather than one. You could compute four separate rates by Method 1 and report the spread. That would be wrong in an interesting way: the four markers are not four independent measurements of the same thing. They are four points on a single cumulative slip history, and they constrain each other.

Two constraints come free with the geology. A fault does not slip backwards, so cumulative displacement can only increase with age. And an older marker cannot carry less offset than a younger one, or the two have been misidentified. Together these say the sequence of points must be monotonic in both coordinates.

Rejection sampling

The method, after Gold and Cowgill (2011) and Zinke and others (2017, 2019), is to enforce that constraint by brute force.

  1. Draw an age and a displacement for every marker, independently.
  2. Keep the realisation only if the ages and the displacements are both strictly increasing.
  3. Otherwise throw the whole realisation away and draw again.
  4. From each accepted path, take the rate over each interval between successive markers.

Discarding the entire realisation, rather than repairing the offending marker, is what makes this correct. The surviving draws are samples from the joint distribution conditioned on the constraint, which is what you wanted. This is the same accept-and-reject loop implemented in Zinke's RISeR toolbox, which is the reference for the method.

Interval rates

Each accepted path gives a rate for every interval between successive markers. Collected across all paths, each interval has its own distribution. This is the actual output of the method: not one slip rate, but a rate for each window the data can resolve.

The acceptance rate is a result

It is tempting to read the acceptance rate as a performance statistic, something to be improved. It is not. It is a measurement of how well your markers agree with one another.

  • Near 100 per cent. The constraint is barely binding. Your ages and offsets are already consistent with a monotonic history, and the analysis is telling you very little you did not know.
  • A few tens of per cent. The constraint is doing real work. Bracketing constraints overlap, and conditioning on monotonicity is genuinely sharpening the result. Both published presets on this page sit here.
  • Single digits. Something is wrong. The surviving draws are a strange, heavily conditioned corner of your input distributions, and any rate computed from them should be treated as a symptom rather than a result. The mispairing page is a worked example.

Report it. A published incremental slip rate without its acceptance rate is missing the diagnostic that tells a reader whether to believe it.

The other reason to prefer intervals

An interval rate is a difference divided by a difference, so any error shared between two neighbouring markers partly cancels. A rate computed from a single marker carries that error in full. This is the quiet argument for incremental analysis over per-marker rates, and it becomes the whole argument on the mispairing page.
