Which number goes in the paper

You have ten thousand slip rates. A paper needs one, with an uncertainty. The choice of how to compress the distribution is not cosmetic: two defensible conventions give different numbers, and one of them moves when you change a setting that has nothing to do with your data.

Three ways to summarise

Mean and standard deviation

The wrong answer, included so it can be dismissed. A skewed distribution has its mean pulled towards the long tail, so the mean is not the most likely value and is not the middle one either. Worse, the interval it implies is symmetric, and the distribution is not.

Percentile interval

Sort the samples and read off the 15.865th, 50th and 84.135th values. That gives a median and a 68.27 per cent interval that is asymmetric in the right direction. It has one large virtue: there is nothing to tune. Two people with the same samples get the same interval, and the answer does not depend on how anyone binned anything.

Its weakness is the mirror of that virtue. Because it takes equal probability from each tail, a long right tail drags the upper bound out further than the shape of the distribution really justifies.

Highest posterior density

Take the narrowest set of values that together hold 68.27 per cent of the probability. Do it by sorting bins by height and accumulating until you have enough. The result tracks the peak, is usually narrower than the percentile interval, and has a real advantage: if the distribution has two separate peaks the method reports two separate clusters instead of pretending the gap between them is plausible.

Its weakness is that it depends on binning and smoothing, which are choices about the plot rather than facts about the data.

The thing to notice

Drag the bins and smoothing sliders. The HPD bounds move. The percentile bounds do not. That is the whole trade-off made physical: HPD gives you a tighter and more representative interval, at the cost of depending on a choice that is not in your data. It is exactly why RISeR implements both and makes you pick.

When the distribution has two peaks

Load the bimodal preset. The percentile interval spans the gap between the two peaks, which asserts that the values in the middle are plausible when the analysis says they are among the least plausible in the range. The HPD method instead reports two clusters and leaves the gap out.

This is not a contrived case. Calibrated radiocarbon ages go bimodal routinely wherever the calibration curve has a plateau, and the rate inherits it. If your rate distribution has two peaks, a single interval of any kind is hiding your main result.

What to report

  1. The median with a percentile interval as the headline number. It is reproducible without tuning, which matters more for a published value than being slightly narrower.
  2. The HPD as well when the distribution is multimodal, or when the percentile interval is visibly dragged by a tail. Report the clusters, not their outer envelope.
  3. Say which you used, and at what confidence level, as a number. Write 68.27 per cent rather than one sigma: for a skewed distribution those are not the same statement.
  4. Plot the distribution. A median and two bounds cannot show bimodality, a fat tail, or a pile-up against a hard bound. The figure can.
  5. Write the errors asymmetrically. 7.0 +1.0 −0.7 mm/yr, not 7.0 ± 0.85. The second discards the shape you went to the trouble of computing.
And the number that is usually missing

For an incremental analysis, report the acceptance rate alongside the interval. Without it a reader cannot tell whether your markers agreed with each other or whether the answer came from a heavily conditioned corner of your inputs. See the incremental page.
