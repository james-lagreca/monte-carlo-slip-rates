Dividing one distribution by another

The oldest calculation in the field: an offset over an age. Done properly it produces a distribution, not a number with an error bar, and the difference matters most exactly when your age is worst.

Your measurement is two distributions

A restored offset is not 12 m. It is a preferred restoration of 12 m, which could defensibly be as little as 10.5 m or as much as 13.5 m, and the preferred value is genuinely more likely than the bounds. That is a distribution, and on this site it is a Beta-PERT, the shape a minimum, preferred and maximum estimate really implies.

An optically stimulated luminescence age is not 4 ka. It is 4.0 ± 0.2 ka, a Gaussian. A calibrated radiocarbon age is not even that: it is whatever shape the calibration curve produced, which is often lumpy and sometimes has two peaks.

SR = D / (t − tanchor) D is the displacement in metres, t the marker age in ka, and tanchor the onset of the displacement clock. Set the anchor to zero for now; the onset of offset page is about why you should not leave it there.

Two ways to answer

The conventional answer divides the central values and propagates the errors to first order:

SR = D̄ / T̄   and   σSR = SR · √( (σD/D̄)² + (σT/T̄)² )

This is not a straw man. It is what most published single-marker slip rates are, and for a well-dated marker it is very nearly right. The Monte Carlo answer instead draws a displacement, draws an age, divides, and repeats ten thousand times or more. The whole method is four lines.

Why the tail is on the right

The rate is a displacement divided by an age. Small ages produce large rates, and there is no upper limit to how large: as the sampled age approaches zero the rate runs away. Large ages produce small rates, but a rate cannot fall below zero. The asymmetry is built into the arithmetic.

So the long right tail of the rate distribution is the short lower tail of the age distribution, stretched. That is why the effect is invisible when the age is well known and severe when it is not, and it is why a Gaussian age whose support reaches zero is a genuine hazard rather than a pedantic one. The engine warns you when that happens.

What to report

The median with an asymmetric interval, written the way it will be read: 3.00 +0.88 −0.57 mm/yr. Not the mean, which the tail drags upward. Not a single ±, which cannot describe a shape that is not symmetric. State the confidence level as a number, 68.27 per cent rather than one sigma, because for a skewed distribution the two are not the same thing.

Which interval to use, and what to do when the distribution has two peaks, is the subject of the reporting page.
