When does the clock start?

A slip rate is a displacement divided by a time. Deciding which time is a question about earthquakes, not about arithmetic, and getting it wrong biases every young marker in the same direction.

The open interval

A marker formed 1.6 ka ago and now carries 3.6 m of offset. The obvious rate is 3.6 divided by 1.6, which is 2.25 mm/yr. Consider what that denominator assumes.

Surface displacement on a fault is not delivered continuously. It arrives in earthquakes. Since the most recent one, the fault has been loading elastically and the marker has recorded nothing at all, because there has been nothing to record. If the last earthquake was 0.4 ka ago, then of the marker's 1.6 ka of existence only 1.2 ka were capable of producing the offset you measured.

Charging the rate for that final 0.4 ka is charging it for time in which no displacement could have accumulated. Following Hatem and others, the denominator should be the time since the most recent event.

SR = Dm / (tm − tMRE) Dm is the offset of the marker, tm its age, and tMRE the age of the most recent surface-rupturing earthquake at the site.

The bias has a closed form

Because the correction only changes the denominator, the size of the error follows immediately:

SRunanchored / SRtrue = 1 − tMRE / tm

So the bias depends only on the ratio of the open interval to the marker's age. It is always negative, meaning unanchored rates always read low, and it grows as markers get younger. For a marker only a few open intervals old the effect is large enough to change a conclusion.

This is worth stating plainly because it inverts an intuition. Young, well-dated markers feel like the most reliable ones: the chronology is tighter and the geomorphology is fresher. They are also the ones this bias hits hardest.

Anchoring is not free

Subtracting an uncertain age from another uncertain age adds their variances. The anchored rate is unbiased but wider, and for the youngest marker the 68 per cent interval roughly doubles in width. That is the honest trade: you have swapped a confident wrong answer for a less confident right one.

It also means the correction should be applied consistently rather than only where it helps. Applying it to your young markers and not your old ones would manufacture an apparent slip-rate change out of a bookkeeping choice.

What to do

State the anchor. Say which earthquake it is, where its age came from, and apply it to every marker at the site including the ones where the correction is negligible. If you have no paleoseismic constraint on the most recent event, say that too, and report the unanchored rate as the lower bound it is.

A caution about very young markers

As a marker's age approaches the age of the most recent event, the denominator approaches zero and the anchored rate runs away to infinity. That is not a numerical artefact to be clipped: it is the method telling you that a marker which has recorded one earthquake, or none, cannot constrain a rate. Set the most recent event slider close to the marker age above and watch the interval become useless.

A rate needs at least two earthquakes between its endpoints before the word means anything. Below that you are not measuring a rate, you are measuring one displacement and dividing it by a poorly known number.
