Choosing a distribution is a scientific claim

Before you can divide two distributions you have to decide what they are. Each shape below says something specific about what you know, and picking one because it is familiar rather than because it is true will quietly bias everything downstream.

What each shape claims

Gaussian

Says the value is a measurement with symmetric error and no hard bounds. Right for a luminescence age, and for most laboratory quantities. Note that this site builds Gaussians on a support of plus or minus four standard deviations, matching RISeR, rather than an infinite one.

The hazard: a Gaussian has tails, and if the lower tail of an age reaches zero then the slip rate it produces is unbounded. The tool warns you when that happens. It is not a rounding problem, it is the model saying the marker might be zero years old.

Boxcar

Says the value lies between two bounds and you have no basis for preferring anything inside them. This is the honest choice when your constraint is stratigraphic bracketing: a unit sits above one dated horizon and below another, and nothing more is known. Both published datasets on this site arrive as boxcars, because that is the form bracketing constraints take.

It is easy to feel that a boxcar throws away information. It does the opposite: choosing a peaked shape instead would add information you do not have.

Triangular

Says there is a preferred value and two hard bounds, and the density falls linearly to zero at each. Simple, and the mean is just the average of the three numbers.

Trapezoid

The shape for a specific and common situation: two restorations of an offset are both defensible and you cannot choose between them. Put the flat top across both, and the falling limbs out to the extreme bounds. The plateau says these are equally likely; the limbs say values beyond are progressively less plausible.

This is a genuinely useful move, because the alternative is to pick one restoration and pretend the other never occurred to you.

Beta-PERT

What a field geologist usually means by minimum, preferred and maximum. Bounded like a triangle but smooth, and it takes the preferred value more seriously than a triangle does. Its shape parameters are

α = 1 + 4(mode − min)/(max − min)     β = 1 + 4(max − mode)/(max − min) The 4 is the standard PERT shape constant, which means α + β = 6 for every PERT, whatever the numbers.

Its moments are closed form, which makes it easy to check a sampler against:

µ = (min + 4·mode + max) / 6     σ = √( (µ − min)(max − µ) / 7 )

Empirical

A table of value and probability pairs. This is how a calibrated radiocarbon age arrives from OxCal, and it is the format RISeR writes. The curve is treated as piecewise linear and sampled by inverting its cumulative distribution exactly. Loading one of these is how you would put a real age onto this site.

Calibrated ages are frequently not unimodal. A calibration plateau can split an age into two peaks, the rate inherits the split, and a single confidence interval will paper over it. The reporting page is about what to do instead.

Does shape matter once the moments are fixed?

It is tempting to think that once you have fixed a mean and a standard deviation, the shape is a detail. Below, a trapezoid and a Gaussian are matched on both, then both are divided by the same age.

The answer is worth being precise about, because it is easy to overstate. For these two shapes the medians agree to three decimal places and the 68 per cent intervals differ by a few per cent in width. That is a small effect, and if it were the whole story you could reasonably ignore it.

It is not the whole story, for two reasons. The bounded shape has hard limits and the Gaussian does not, so they diverge in the tails rather than in the middle, and it is the tails that matter when you divide. And the shapes that genuinely change an answer are the ones whose moments do not match a Gaussian's: a boxcar where you assumed a peak, or a bimodal calibrated age summarised as a mean and a sigma. Those are not small effects at all.

Rule of thumb

Pick the shape that describes how you actually know the number. Bracketing constraints give a boxcar. A laboratory measurement gives a Gaussian. A field restoration with a preferred value gives a PERT or a triangle. Two competing restorations give a trapezoid. A calibrated age gives whatever the calibration produced, and you should carry it rather than summarising it as a mean and a sigma.
