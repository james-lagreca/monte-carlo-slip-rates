Monte Carlo slip rates

You have one offset marker and one date. What is the slip rate, and how wrong could it be? This site works through the two Monte Carlo methods that answer that question, and the field-geology traps that make both of them lie to you.

A geologic slip rate is a displacement divided by a time. Neither number is known exactly. The displacement is a restoration with a preferred value and defensible bounds; the age is a laboratory measurement with its own distribution, and if it is a calibrated radiocarbon date it may not even be unimodal.

Dividing one distribution by another is not the same as dividing two numbers and carrying an error bar along. It is the difference between a result you can defend and one that is quietly wrong in a known direction.

Units, stated once

Ages are in ka, displacements in m, rates in mm/yr. One metre per thousand years is one millimetre per year exactly, so metres divided by ka is already mm/yr. There is no unit conversion anywhere on this site, and that is not an oversight.

The two methods

Method 1

One dated marker

Sample a displacement, sample an age, divide, repeat. The result is a rate distribution, and it is skewed. Why plus-or-minus fails, and what to report instead.

 Method 2

Incremental analysis

Several dated markers record a slip history, not a rate. Rejection sampling, the path fan, interval rates, and why the acceptance rate is a result rather than a nuisance.

Two more pages sit alongside each method. Distributions covers the shapes you can put on a measurement and what each one claims. Reporting covers which summary of a skewed rate belongs in a paper.

The traps

Both methods are arithmetic, and arithmetic does what it is told. The two pages below are about the geology that decides whether what it is told is true.

Trap 1

When does the clock start?

Strain has accumulated since the last earthquake but nothing has been recorded at the surface. Divide by the marker's full age and you under-read the rate, worst for the youngest markers.

 Trap 2

Dating the wrong event

Offsets accumulate from when a channel forms. Luminescence dates its fill, near abandonment. Pair the two and the statistics will not save you, but they will tell you it happened.

A first look

Below is the whole of Method 1 in one figure. A displacement of PERT(10.5, 12.0, 13.5) m over an age of 4.0 ka, with only the age uncertainty under your control. Drag the slider up and watch a near-symmetric rate distribution grow a tail to the right.

The rate distribution, as the age gets worse

The displacement never changes. Everything you see is the age uncertainty propagating through a division.

With an age of 4.00 ± 0.20 ka the rate is 3.00 mm/yr with a 68 per cent interval of 2.80 to 3.22, near enough symmetric. Raise the age uncertainty to ± 0.90 ka and the same displacement gives 3.00 mm/yr with an interval of 2.43 to 3.88, and a 95 per cent upper bound of 5.41. How to read every figure here

Colours mean the same thing on every page, and they are the colours used in the corresponding publication figures.

ColourMeans
BlueObserved data. Marker boxes, input distributions.
Red-brownThe naive estimate: the thing being corrected.
GreenThe corrected or preferred result, and known ground truth.
GreyMonte Carlo paths, the incremental benchmark.
Dark redRejection, failure, a result that cannot be true.

Because those colours are close in lightness, which is right for print figures separated into panels and wrong for lines overlaid on one axis, every series also carries its own dash pattern and is labelled where it is drawn rather than in a key.

About the data

Every teaching dataset here is synthetic. The synthetic cases are forward-modelled from a stated answer, so you can check whether an estimator recovers the number it was built from. Two further presets carry displacement and age brackets digitised from published studies, are labelled as published, and are cited on the references page. No unpublished field measurement appears anywhere on this site.
